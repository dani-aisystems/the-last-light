3. Personality — five defining traits
1. Meticulous

He checks the lamp, fuel supply and rotation mechanism in the same order every watch. If interrupted, he returns to the beginning rather than trusting his memory. His records are carefully ruled and timed, with corrections marked rather than casually overwritten.

2. Dryly humorous

When the weather drives rain beneath the door, he remarks, "At least we needn't carry the water upstairs." He never laughs at his own jokes and almost never delivers one when another man is genuinely frightened.

3. Guarded

He answers personal questions with practical information. If someone asks whether he remembers the wreck, he says, "I was here." He will discuss the weather that night before he discusses himself.

4. Patient with the inexperienced

He gives a young skipper time to repeat a position report without embarrassment. He never mistakes inexperience for stupidity. When danger is close, he becomes more precise, not louder.

5. Quietly controlling

He dislikes leaving a repair half-finished and is reluctant to entrust a difficult task to another person. He checks equipment already checked by others. His fear of error has become a habit of mistrust, including mistrust of himself.

4. Motivation tonight / deeper want
Immediate motivation: Keep the North Point light operating through the gale. Answer calls correctly. Protect the vessels approaching the dangerous waters around East Gut. Get through his watch without another death.

Conscious motivation: Preserve the station's reputation and his father's memory without having to speak about the old disaster.

Deeper want: He wants to believe he can still be a good man without first having to acknowledge what he did as a frightened young one.

He does not seek forgiveness at the beginning of the game. Forgiveness would require admitting there is something to forgive.

His work has become an argument he makes to himself, each night: The lamp is burning now. I am here now.

The game challenges the sufficiency of that argument.

5. Fear
Callum has two connected fears.

Becoming his father: Not merely drinking or sleeping on duty — Callum keeps an especially strict watch against both — but becoming a man who chooses the preservation of his own dignity over another person's life.

He fears that Iain's weakness was not an exceptional failure. He fears it was something ordinary in the family, waiting for the correct circumstances.

Being found out: Exposure would mean that the condemned skipper was blamed for circumstances Callum knew had been falsified. It would expose Callum's role in maintaining that account for eighteen years.

He fears disgrace, loss of his position, and the judgement of the people who trust his word. More privately, he fears that if his father's reputation is stripped away, there will be nothing left of their relationship except coercion.

His worst fear is not that somebody will call him a liar.

It is that they will be right.

6. What he hides, and what he knows but has not said
What he knows
At approximately 02:01 on 15 November 1953, he woke and relit the lamp.

When he woke, the light had already failed; he did not personally witness the entire sequence leading to its failure.

He did not wake in time to prevent the Mary Ellen from striking East Gut at 01:58.

At 03:00, Iain dictated an official entry which placed the failure after the wreck and shifted blame towards Ross.

Callum wrote the entry himself, knowingly.

He has continued to let the official account stand.

What he suspects, but cannot honestly claim to have witnessed
He suspects his father had been drinking and failed to keep watch. He knows enough about Iain's behaviour that night to doubt the defence his father constructed, but his own firsthand observations do not establish every minute of the failure.

This distinction matters. In an honest account, Callum must separate what he saw, what he wrote and what he has concluded since.

What he does not know at the opening
He does not know that Alasdair Ross survived, has lived as Sandy Morrison in Govan, or is the person who will eventually arrive at his door.

He also does not know everything Morag's original radio record could prove. The recorded 01:52 Mayday is independent evidence of an unlit North Point before the grounding.

How the secret manifests
He keeps the 1953 log where it belongs, not hidden under a floorboard. Concealing it would require treating it as evidence; leaving it on the shelf allows him to pretend it is just another record.

He will inspect a damaged mechanical tooth without hesitation. He is slower to open a book bearing his own handwriting.

8. Voice
Callum's spoken manner is economical, local without theatrical dialect, and dependent on context. He seldom explains a feeling directly. When upset, he shortens his sentences rather than lengthening them.

Speech rhythm
Usually two or three short clauses, sometimes just one.

Plain statements, with a small pause before anything personally difficult.

Questions are practical: "Where are you now?" rather than "How do you feel?"

When under pressure, his grammar remains orderly but his answers become more abrupt.

His rare admissions often begin with a precise factual correction.

Vocabulary and idiom
"Aye", "right enough", "mind", "steady", "that'll do", "not yet", "I reckon", "you'll be wanting", "there's no need".

Use light Hebridean cadence without phonetic accent spellings or Gaelic inserted for atmosphere. He should sound like an educated, locally rooted working man, not a collection of regional expressions.

Radio procedure
In 1971 he uses voice radio, not modern digital signalling. He identifies the station, makes calls intelligible, reads back critical information and distinguishes ordinary traffic from urgency or distress.

Channel 16 is used for distress, safety and calling. Routine extended exchanges should move to an agreed working channel when appropriate, rather than occupying the distress frequency. During an emergency, he gives relevant information clearly, keeps transmissions short and does not invent an authority he does not possess.

North Point is a lighthouse, not a Coastguard rescue coordination station. Callum may relay information or help a vessel, but should not casually speak as though he commands a rescue service.

Typical transmission:

"Elspeth Ross, North Point Light. Say your position. Over."

He says over when he expects a reply, out when the exchange is finished. Never "over and out".

Verbal tics
He says "Right" when accepting something difficult. "Aye" may indicate agreement, but sometimes merely confirms that he has heard. When displeased, he repeats a factual question rather than issuing a personal accusation.

What he would never say: "I've got this", "We're all in this together", "You need to trust me", "Everything happens for a reason", or grand pronouncements about destiny, ghosts, guilt and the sea.

He does not describe himself as haunted.

He knows better than to promise that a vessel will be safe.

10. Arc across the night, beat by beat
The ordinary watch — competence as protection

Callum begins with established duties. His routine makes him appear dependable. The player learns the room through his disciplined relationship with its equipment. He corrects small deviations without comment.

The first unsettling call — a name returns

A vessel calls herself Mary Ellen. His immediate instinct is to verify rather than react. The player can hear the difference between professional caution and personal alarm.

The past enters the room — records become evidence

Questions, old objects and the 1953 log establish that the official account is not a neutral memory. The player may choose guarded or candid responses, but the handwriting makes Callum's involvement undeniable.

The knock — the man behind the record

An unlit boat comes below the light. The Visitor arrives. Callum must confront a living Alasdair Ross rather than the dead skipper named in the Inquiry.

The confrontation — obedience examined

Ross challenges the official account. Callum can defend his father's authority or acknowledge that obedience did not make the false entry true. His composure remains largely intact, but his choice of words becomes increasingly revealing.

The second failure — duty without evasion

Elspeth's vessel loses steering. The rotation mechanism jams again at 046°. Callum and Ross keep the light turning by hand. Whatever has been said between them, both understand that a living crew comes first.

Crucially, this is not a symbolic act performed instead of rescue. The immediate machinery and maritime emergency must remain credible, urgent and practical.

After the danger — the cost of speaking

With the immediate threat addressed, Callum must decide what account to transmit. The radio is no longer only an instrument for passing warnings; it is the means by which his private decision becomes part of the public record.

Ending A — truth

He states plainly what he wrote, what was false, and what he knows about the timing. He does not demand forgiveness, and the game does not promise it. His father's name is exposed to scrutiny; Callum accepts that consequence rather than transferring it to another man.

Ending B — the protective lie

He preserves the false account or declines to correct it. He can still save Elspeth's vessel and perform his duties well. That is what makes the ending painful: his courage in the present does not undo his choice to remain silent about the past.

The essential arc is not coward becomes hero. Callum is already capable of genuine courage and kindness. The question is whether he will apply those qualities to a truth that threatens him personally.

1. Summary
Morag Fraser is a precise, stubborn radio officer at Stornoway Coastguard and Callum Mackay's oldest friend, whose calm voice has guided frightened mariners through emergencies for more than twenty years. She has kept a pencilled record of the 1953 Mary Ellen Mayday that contradicts the official account, and tonight she must decide whether protecting Callum means continuing to spare him or finally demanding an answer.

2. Background and life timeline
Morag was born in 1927 and grew up in Tarbert, Harris, where she knew Callum Mackay from school. They were never especially demonstrative friends. They shared books, helped each other with arithmetic, walked stretches of the same road home, and developed a familiarity that survived years of separation.

She entered radio work young. She had a clear speaking voice, an excellent ear for weak transmissions and the patience to distinguish a genuine message from interference. She learned that a single incorrect figure could send help to the wrong place.

Her marriage was a happy, ordinary one, never idealised in retrospect. Her husband worked aboard a drifter. She understood the risks but resisted treating loss as inevitable.

3. Personality — five defining traits
1. Precise

When a vessel gives an uncertain bearing, she asks for the bearing again before entering it. She reads figures back slowly and does not substitute a plausible guess for a missing detail. Her pencil remains poised until she has enough to write.

2. Warm beneath formality

After midnight, when traffic is light, her manner softens. She remembers who has been working too long and who has a young family waiting ashore. With Callum, she may ask whether he has eaten, but only after the necessary business is finished.

3. Stubborn

She refuses to discard her 1953 notes, despite years without any apparent prospect of challenging the Inquiry. When challenged about their accuracy, she checks them again rather than arguing from pride.

4. Emotionally disciplined

The news of her husband's death taught her the practical cruelty of waiting for information. During emergencies she avoids speculation, even when someone asks her for reassurance she cannot give.

5. Quietly confrontational

She seldom raises her voice. Instead, she repeats a question until the person answering has either supplied the fact or plainly refused to do so. Her silences can be more uncomfortable than anger.

4. Motivation tonight / deeper want
Immediate motivation: Maintain reliable radio contact, establish vessel positions and coordinate the necessary Coastguard response to dangerous conditions around North Point.

When Elspeth's vessel loses steering, Morag's professional responsibility is unambiguous: obtain the vessel's position and condition, alert and coordinate available rescue resources through the Coastguard's procedures, and keep the communications orderly.

Personal motivation: Find out why the name Mary Ellen has returned, and whether the events now unfolding expose the flaw in the official 1953 account.

Deeper want: Morag wants to stop treating loyalty and truth as opposing duties.

She has spent much of her life being useful to other people. She could not bring her husband home, could not bring the seven men of the Mary Ellen home, and could not make the old Inquiry agree with what she wrote.

She wants her observations to have mattered.

Not because she needs to be proven clever or vindicated, but because a man was blamed, a family was shamed, and seven other families were left with an account she has never been able to reconcile with her record.

Her deeper need is to believe that speaking at the right time can still do some good.

5. Fear
Morag's defining fear has two incompatible halves.

What if she was wrong?

If her clock was inaccurate, if she misheard the transmission or if she made a mistake recording the time, then challenging the 1954 Inquiry could inflict another injustice on a family already marked by disaster.

The fact that she trusts herself professionally does not make this easier. It makes the possibility of error more serious.

What if she was right?

If the Mary Ellen reported an unlit North Point at 01:52, six minutes before the grounding, then the official chronology is false.

That would mean Callum, her oldest friend, has either known something essential or lived beside a lie without asking the questions she has asked herself for eighteen years.

She fears losing him. She fears discovering that their friendship has depended on her willingness not to know.

The second fear is worse because it demands action.

6. What she hides, and what she knows but has not said
Her private 1953 pencilled notes
Morag kept a small paper notebook alongside her formal working records. Its pages contain reception times, short summaries and occasional direct phrases from transmissions.

It is not an official verbatim transcript. It is her contemporaneous working note, with its evidential strength coming from the timestamp and the distinctive phrase she recorded.

PROP TRANSCRIPTION — EXACT IN-GAME TEXT

14/15 Nov. 1953 — night watch

01.52  MAYDAY — MARY ELLEN
       SY 214. Ross speaking.
       "no light at North Point"
       Position near East Gut.
       Heavy weather. Signal poor.

       Time checked against station clock.

01.58  Report of grounding — East Gut.

       Light not seen before impact?

Keep this page.

This is the proposed canonical wording for the fictional prop. The precisely established facts are the 01:52 Mayday, the vessel identity and the report of no light. The other shorthand is added as dramatic detail, not as a claim that Morag possesses a complete recorded transmission.

Physical appearance of the notes
A small, soft-covered notebook, approximately pocket size, with a dark faded cover and rounded corners.

Yellowed, thin ruled paper with graphite rubbed into the crease.

A firm, compact hand with upright lettering and sharply formed numerals.

The time 01.52 written clearly, not revised or overwritten.

A dark pencil mark beside "no light at North Point", as though Morag later returned to it.

The final instruction, "Keep this page", added more heavily than the original notes, without a date.

Unlike Callum, who wrote a complete false official entry in ink, Morag wrote a rough private record in pencil.

That difference should be evident visually.

What she knows
She received the Mayday at 01:52 and recorded the absence of the North Point light. She knows the official chronology places the light's failure after the wreck, which occurred at 01:58.

She knows Callum was at the station that night and that Iain Mackay was principal keeper.

She does not initially know that Iain dictated a false entry, that Callum physically wrote it under his father's instruction, or that Ross survived the wreck.

What she has not said
She has never been fully candid with Callum about how often she has revisited those times. Her friendship has made her hesitant to demand an explanation, and her professional uncertainty has offered a reason to defer the question.

Her silence should not be mistaken for proof that she knowingly participated in a cover-up. She suspects the account is wrong; she does not know the full mechanism of the lie.

8. Voice
Morag's voice is recognised through the contrast between two registers: formal, disciplined radio language and a quieter, more personal tone.

Speech rhythm
On air, she speaks in short, complete sentences. Critical words and numbers are repeated when necessary. She does not waste airtime on speculation.

Off-procedure, especially late at night with Callum, her sentences lengthen slightly. Her wit becomes warmer, and questions about food, sleep or the weather serve as indirect expressions of care.

When worried, she begins using first names.

"North Point Light" becomes "Callum".

"Ross" becomes "Elspeth".

This should be unusual enough for the player to notice.

Vocabulary and idiom
Natural phrases include "Aye", "Right then", "That's understood", "Say again", "Very well", "Mind yourself", "I'll need that once more", and "You're not answering me".

She is not given to flowery observations about the sea. Her language is rooted in work, family and the practical understanding of people who live with poor weather.

Radio habits
Morag identifies her station, confirms essential information and keeps distress traffic clear. In the 1971 scenes, Channel 16 provides the distress and calling context established by the game; she can arrange working communications as appropriate. She does not use modern digital distress procedures.

A typical transmission:

"North Point Light, Stornoway Coastguard. Say again your last message. Over."

During a real emergency, she is not so absorbed in personal history that she abandons procedure. If Callum attempts an extended confession while Elspeth remains in danger, Morag directs him back to the rescue.

Historical production note: do not depict the 1953 Mayday as a modern Channel 16 digital transmission. Maritime radio and Coastguard VHF networks were evolving across these decades; a period source describes the expansion of British Coastguard VHF equipment during the 1960s.

Verbal tics
She sometimes begins a difficult personal question with "Now". When unconvinced, she says "Is that so?" without sarcasm.

Her most revealing habit is asking for the same figure twice.

What she would never say: "Trust your instincts", "We'll figure this out together", "I know exactly how you feel", or a dramatic speech about the dead demanding justice.

She does not call herself a widow to win an argument.

9. Ten sample lines across moods
Morag is not player-controlled, but her lines can respond to Callum's protective or honest choices. Each pair is an alternate Morag response.

01 · CALM — OPENING RADIO CONTACT

Protective route: "North Point Light, Stornoway Coastguard. Receiving you clearly. Over."

Honest route: "North Point Light, Stornoway Coastguard. All well at your end, Callum? Over."

02 · CALM — A QUIET MOMENT AFTER MIDNIGHT

Protective route: "You'll have had your tea, I suppose. Though I wouldn't wager on it."

Honest route: "You've been at that machinery again, haven't you? I can hear it in your answers."

03 · WORRIED — MARY ELLEN CALLS

Protective route: "Repeat that vessel's name, please. Over."

Honest route: "Callum. You heard the name as well as I did."

04 · WORRIED — SHE RETRIEVES HER NOTES

Protective route: "I may have made an error. I have to allow for that."

Honest route: "I wrote the time down while he was speaking. One fifty-two. It's there in pencil."

05 · CONFRONTATION — CHAPTER 3, THE TIMES

Protective route: "Then tell me why the Inquiry has a different time."

Honest route: "He said there was no light. Six minutes before the wreck. Tell me how that can be."

06 · ANGRY — CALLUM EVADES HER QUESTION

Protective route: "I asked you a question, Callum. I haven't asked you to defend your father."

Honest route: "You could have told me. All these years, you could have told me."

07 · ANGRY — THE FALSE ENTRY

Protective route: "An official entry isn't made true by sitting in a book."

Honest route: "Your handwriting. His words. Aye, I understand the difference. It doesn't make the entry true."

08 · URGENT — ELS PETH LOSES STEERING

Protective route: "Elspeth Ross, Stornoway Coastguard. Confirm steering lost. Give your present position. Over."

Honest route: "Elspeth, listen carefully. I need your position again, and your heading. Over."

09 · BREAKING — THE RESCUE IS UNDER WAY

Protective route: "Callum, keep that light turning. The rest can be dealt with afterwards."

Honest route: "Callum, I know what you're trying to tell me. Keep the light turning first."

10 · AFTERMATH — THE FINAL DECISION

Protective route: "Very well. I'll record what you've said."

Honest route: "I have your statement, Callum. And I still have my notes."

Morag never needs to forgive Callum during the ending. In the honest route, a small acknowledgment that she has heard him is enough.

10. Arc across the night, beat by beat
Chapter 1 — The familiar voice

Morag introduces the Coastguard's presence, providing reliable information and occasional warmth. The player recognises that Callum and Morag have known each other for years without needing an explanatory conversation.

Chapter 2 — An impossible name

When Mary Ellen is heard, Morag responds with professional verification. She is unsettled but does not yet accuse anyone. The player hears her use Callum's first name earlier than usual.

Chapter 3 — The confrontation over time

She retrieves her 1953 notebook and places its 01:52 entry against the official account. She asks Callum to reconcile the discrepancy. If he evades, her questions become firmer. If he admits what he wrote, she must confront the fact that her oldest friend withheld something essential.
